Brownian Motion

In our treatment of the random walk we learnt: \[\langle x_S^2 \rangle = S^2n\] where S is the step length, n the number of steps and \(\langle x_s^2 \rangle\) the average displacement squared, working in only one dimension for now. We want to model brownian motion - the random motion of particles due to collisions with the molecules of the surrounding liquid or gas. The number of steps is proportional to the time taken so: \[\langle x_S^2 \rangle = \alpha t\] for some constant \(\alpha\)\, we want to find the value of. The random forces on the particle due to brownian motion \(F_x\)\, will cause acceleration but will also have a drag force against them due to other collisions when the particle we consider moves through the medium. We will model the drag as proportional to \(v\) or \(\frac{dx}{dt}\) so: \[F_x = m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \mu \frac{dx}{dt}\] \(\mu\)\, is readily determined experimentally, for example it could be done for a skydiver falling through the air by drawing their velocity-time graph and dividing their terminal velocity by their weight. Since there are derivatives we will first find \(\langle \frac{dx^2}{dt} \rangle\) and integrate it to get \(\langle x^2 \rangle\). Note that the average kinetic energy is proportional to temperature: \[\langle \frac{1}{2}m(\frac{dx}{dt})^2 \rangle = \frac{kT}{2}\] k is Boltzmann's constant. The chain rule for differentiation implies: \[\langle \frac{dx^2}{dt} \rangle = \langle 2x\frac{dx}{dt} \rangle \] We need to look for a position times velocity in our original force equation. Averaging both sides of the equation times \(x)\: \[\langle xF_x \rangle = m\langle x\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}\rangle + \mu \langle x\frac{dx}{dt}\rangle\] Here is the sneaky part. The product rule for differentiation means we can write that: \[x\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = \frac{d}{dt}(x\frac{dx}{dt}) - (\frac{dx}{dt})^2 \] so since \(\langle xF_x \rangle = 0)\ because the force is random \[\langle\frac{d}{dt}(x\frac{dx}{dt})\rangle - \langle(\frac{dx}{dt})^2\rangle = -\frac{\mu}{m}\langle x\frac{dx}{dt}\rangle \] but \[\langle (\frac{dx}{dt})^2 \rangle =\frac{ kT}{m}\] we can argue \(\langle\frac{d}{dt}(x\frac{dx}{dt})\rangle)\ must be zero since position and velocity are not time dependent, they are random at all times. This gives: \\\[\mu\langle x\frac{dx}{dt}\rangle = 2kT\] \[\langle \frac{dx^2}{dt} \rangle = \frac{2kT}{\mu}\] and finally: \[\langle x^2\rangle = \frac{2kTt}{\mu}\] In three dimensions randomness means \[\langle x^2\rangle=\langle y^2\rangle=\langle z^2\rangle\] and so \[\langle R^2\rangle = 3\langle x^2 \rangle = \frac{6kTt}{\mu}\]
